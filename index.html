It took twenty-five years to coil.

Before it was a flag, the rattlesnake was a joke, a warning and a woodcut in a Philadelphia newspaper. This is how it got from there to a yellow field.

The lines quoted in the film come from one letter, printed in Philadelphia at the end of 1775.

  1. 17519 May, Philadelphia

    A joke with teeth

    Britain was shipping convicted felons to its American colonies. Benjamin Franklin’s Pennsylvania Gazette suggested the colonies send rattlesnakes back in return.

  2. 17549 May, Philadelphia

    Join, or Die

    Franklin printed a snake cut into eight pieces, each one a colony or region, to argue they had to act together. Folklore held that a cut snake could live again if its pieces were joined before sunset.

  3. 1775Philadelphia

    Painted on a drum

    The Continental Marines raised in Philadelphia that winter carried a drum painted with a rattlesnake, “with this modest motto under it, ‘Don’t tread on me.’”

  4. 1775December, Philadelphia

    A standard for the navy

    Christopher Gadsden of South Carolina sat on the committee fitting out the first Continental Navy. He gave Commodore Esek Hopkins a yellow rattlesnake flag to fly as his personal standard aboard the flagship Alfred.

  5. 177527 December, Philadelphia

    The case for the rattlesnake

    A letter signed “An American Guesser,” widely attributed to Franklin, argued that the rattlesnake was the right emblem for America: watchful, slow to start a fight and impossible to make quit.

    Pennsylvania Journal

  6. 17769 February, Charleston

    Raised at home

    Gadsden presented a copy to the South Carolina Provincial Congress. Its journal records “a yellow field, with a lively representation of a rattlesnake in the middle, in the attitude of going to strike.”

The snake has a mascot now.

Somewhere between 1775 and the group chat, the snake picked up a sidekick. Gadsden cap on, white goggles down, and a tank top with one piece of advice. Deadpan, poolside and impossible to tread on.
